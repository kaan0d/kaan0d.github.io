--- title: environment-aware pathfinding: the unit that walked around a wall it could have broken date: 2026-09-21 label: Sep 2026 ---

in games where a crowd of units attacks a base, there is a familiar mistake: a wall stands between a unit and its target, a long way around exists, and the unit takes it, even though breaking one cell of the wall would have been much faster. I am building a browser demo that shows this mistake and fixes it. the fix is to treat the environment as something the planner can weigh in seconds, not as a fixed map of obstacles. this post grows with every stage, so it is a work log more than a summary. stage 1 built the baseline: the mistake, reproduced on purpose. stage 2 built the planner that decides by time, for one unit at a time. stage 3 made it work for a crowd: units that stand together share one plan, and the number of places around a wall decides how many of them can hit it. stages 4 to 6 turn all of that into a demo you can open: two panels, an editor, nine maps, a debug view.

the plan

the demo runs two algorithms side by side on the same map, with the same units and the same deploy events. the left one is the classic behavior: pick the building that is closest in a straight line, treat walls as impassable, walk around if a path exists, and hit the nearest wall only when there is none. the right one, built in the next stages, picks its target and route by total time. an empty cell costs its length divided by the unit's speed. a wall cell costs that plus its hit points divided by the unit's damage per second. one number, seconds, so a strong unit and a weak one can reach different decisions on the same wall.

the classic AI here is my model of that behavior, not a claim about how any particular game is coded. what matters for the demo is that it is a reasonable, simple rule with a visible failure.

one more thing about real games, and it comes from playing and watching them, not from reading their code: many of them do have a rule for this. when the way around gets very long, the unit gives up on it and attacks the wall. but the rule is usually a fixed limit (a path longer than some number of cells, or no path at all), not a comparison of what breaking the wall would cost against what walking would cost. so it fires at the wrong moment: a unit walks a long way around a wall that a few hits would remove, or it starts on a thick wall right next to a short way around. either way time is lost. the extra searching, finding a long path and then dropping it, costs computing time too, which matters when there are many units. I have not measured any real game, so this is the motivation for the project and not a result of it. the classic AI in the demo is the simplest form of the behavior, breaking only when no path exists. a version with a length limit is not built. if it turns out that the limit version is the fairer opponent, it would be a third planner next to the two I have.

the project is TypeScript with Vite and Vitest, and the simulation is separate from any drawing: src/sim has no DOM and no rendering library, runs on a fixed step of 1/30 s, and has no randomness in it, so the same scenario always gives the same result. the browser layer comes in stage 4.

what stage 1 builds

  • a grid with walls of five levels (100 to 2,000 hit points), and buildings of 1×1, 2×2 and 3×3 cells that are obstacles for routing but can be attacked.
  • units with a speed, damage per second and hit points, and a small state machine: not spawned, moving, waiting, attacking, done.
  • melee combat with one rule that shapes everything else: a cell can hold at most one attacker, and attackers stay in place until the target dies. units may overlap while walking, but they cannot share an attack position.
  • an event log (deploy, wall destroyed, building destroyed, unit finished) and statistics, so two runs can be compared exactly.
  • the classic AI, and a way to push a plan into a unit from outside, which the tests use to show what the better decision would have been.

the mistake, measured

the test map is 15 by 20 cells. a vertical wall of 18 cells (level 1, 100 hit points) splits it, with a gap only at the bottom. a depot (300 hit points) sits behind the wall, and one balanced unit (2.5 cells per second, 50 damage per second) starts on the other side, 3 cells from the wall.

filled cells are wall, the outlined cell on the right of each wall is the depot, the dot is where the unit starts. in the right panel the wall cell that gets broken is drawn as an outline. both routes come from the simulation, not from a drawing tool.

the depot itself takes 6 s in both runs (300 hit points at 50 damage per second), so the whole difference is the trip: 13.6 s of walking around the wall against 3.7 s of walking and hitting one wall cell. the direct plan is about half the time.

one honest caveat. the "direct" run is not a smarter algorithm. it is a route I wrote by hand and pushed into the unit with forcePlan, to prove that a faster plan exists on this map and that the simulation can execute a plan that goes through a wall. deciding that automatically is stage 2. and the answer is not always "break it": a level 5 wall has 2,000 hit points, which is 40 s of hitting for this unit, longer than the whole detour. the same map with a stronger wall should make the classic AI right. at the end of stage 1 I had not run that; stage 2 did, and the table further down has it.

how the classic AI decides

it picks the building whose center is closest in a straight line, then searches for the cheapest route over empty cells to any free cell next to that building. if a route exists, it walks it. if it does not, because the building is sealed in, it looks at the cells the unit can reach, finds the walls touching that region, and hits the one closest to the target building. when the wall falls it plans again, and the route now exists. when a building falls, it moves on to the next nearest one.

the route search is a plain uniform-cost search, not A*. the stage plan asked for A*, and I left the heuristic out on purpose: the search has to compute the distance to every cell anyway, because that same table tells the AI which cells are reachable when there is no path, and on a 40 by 28 grid it costs nothing. the comment in the code names the ceiling (about 100 by 100) and the upgrade.

units also spread out. if five units all chose the nearest attack position of a building, four would queue on one cell while others stayed free. so when the classic AI picks a position it skips cells that another unit is attacking from or heading to, and only falls back to a shared one when every position is taken. a unit that ends up waiting resumes as soon as its target dies. a test with three units and a depot that has exactly one free position checks that there is never more than one attacker on it, that someone does wait, and that all three finish.

rules that are easy to get wrong

movement is 8-directional, and a unit may not cut a corner: a diagonal step is allowed only if both cells next to it are free. otherwise routes would slip between two diagonal walls and the wall would leak. a 3 by 3 test puts a wall at one cell and checks that the diagonal past it is refused in both directions.

an attack position is any free cell among the 8 neighbors of the target's footprint, diagonals included. the plan said only "neighbor", so this is my choice. it is one function in grid.ts, and it changes how many units can hit a 1×1 wall at once (8, or 4 if diagonals do not count).

the other rule is about where knowledge lives. the walls, buildings and empty ground are not compared by name anywhere in the simulation except one file, which holds a small table of what each kind does:

export const CellKind = { empty: 0, wall: 1, building: 2 } as const const REGISTRY: readonly EnvironmentKind[] = [ { traversal: 'passable', moveCost: 1, targetable: false }, // empty { traversal: 'breakable', moveCost: 1, targetable: true }, // wall { traversal: 'blocked', moveCost: 1, targetable: true }, // building ]

the planner and the movement code ask "is this cell passable, breakable or blocked" and never "is this a wall". the point is that a new kind of terrain, say mud that costs double, can be added in one place. stage 2 tests exactly that. a test scans the source of src/sim and fails if any other file compares against a cell kind.

what stage 1 left open

at the end of stage 1 there was exactly one AI, and it replanned only when its own target was destroyed. units did not react to a wall that someone else broke. a unit walked at its own speed whatever the ground was, because every kind of cell cost 1. the direct plan in the table was handwritten, not computed. the tests covered three small maps, not a full 40 by 28 base, and nothing had run in a browser. stage 2 fixes the first four of those.

stage 2: the same map, planned by time

the time-cost planner has one job: for a single unit, find the building and the route with the smallest total time, where total time is walking, plus breaking every wall on the way, plus destroying the building. it knows nothing about squads yet, so damage does not stack and the number of attack positions does not limit it. that is stage 3. its first test is the L-corner map from above: with the level 1 wall it finishes in 9.70 s, exactly the time of the route I had written by hand, and the 15 tests from stage 1, including the 19.57 s classic run, did not change.

a wall is a cell with a price

the cost model is one function. walking into a cell costs the step length times the cell's moveCost divided by the unit's speed. if the cell is breakable, it also costs the wall's hit points divided by the unit's damage per second. buildings are never entered, they are approached.

export function enterCost(grid: Grid, cell: number, stepLength: number, speed: number, dps: number): number { const kind = grid.kind[cell] const walk = (stepLength * moveCostOf(kind)) / speed return traversalOf(kind) === 'breakable' ? walk + grid.hp[cell] / dps : walk }

this is where the rule from stage 1 pays off. the function asks the registry whether a cell is breakable and what it costs to cross. it never asks whether the cell is a wall.

one table for the whole map

running a search per unit and per building would be slow and would not compare buildings fairly. instead the planner runs one search backwards. it starts from the free cells around every living building, each valued at the time that building takes to destroy (hit points over damage per second), and floods outward with Dijkstra's algorithm. every cell ends up holding one number: the seconds a unit standing there needs to finish its cheapest building. next to that number it stores the next cell to step to and which building the path ends at.

while (heap.size > 0) { const cell = heap.pop() if (heap.lastKey > value[cell]) continue for (let dir = 0; dir < DIR_DX.length; dir++) { const from = grid.stepTarget(cell, dir, true) if (from < 0) continue const candidate = value[cell] + enterCost(grid, cell, DIR_COST[dir], speed, dps) if (candidate >= value[from]) continue value[from] = candidate next[from] = cell target[from] = target[cell] heap.push(candidate, from) } }

a unit reads its answer by looking at its own cell and following the next pointers. picking the best building and the best route is the same lookup, so a big tough building and a small one are compared on the same scale for free. wall cells are part of the table like any other cell, which is why a route can cross several walls in a row. a cell right next to a building may itself be a wall: the unit breaks it, steps in, and hits the building from there.

the two panels below are that table for the L-corner map, for the same balanced unit. brighter cells are closer to finishing. with the level 1 wall the brightness flows straight through the wall; with level 5 it flows down and around, and the wall is not part of any route. the line is the route the unit takes from the dot.

flow field for a balanced unit, level 1 wall on the left and level 5 on the right. grey cells are wall, the outlined cell is the depot. only the route changes between the two panels.

two details I had to decide. the field is computed per unit, not per map, because speed and damage are part of the price: the same wall costs 5 s to a fast unit and 0.67 s to a heavy one. and a route may not step diagonally past a wall, the same corner rule as the classic AI, so a route cannot slip between two walls that touch at a corner.

deciding again when the world changes

a plan made at second 0 goes stale when another unit breaks a wall or a building falls. after every destruction, each unit that is walking or waiting asks the planner again. a unit that has started hitting something never does: it finishes what it began. there is also a hook for the editor, notifyMapChanged(), that a later stage calls after the player edits the map.

asking again all the time would make units dither between two routes of almost the same length, so a new plan replaces the current one only if it is at least 10% faster than what remains of the current one, both measured from the unit's exact position. the 10% comes from the stage plan and I have not tuned it. two tests pin it down. a unit has a route through one gap of a long wall when a second gap opens: when the new route would take 95.4% of the old time it keeps its plan, at 76.9% it switches. with the threshold set to 0 the first test fails and the second still passes, so that setting really is the difference.

the other test is the one I wanted from the start. two depots sit behind a level 2 wall. a heavy unit breaks the wall at 3.03 s. a fast unit that deployed at 1.4 s had planned the detour (28 cells, 21.96 s estimated, no walls), because for it a 250 hit point wall is 12.5 s of hitting. when the hole appears it changes its plan and walks through it. total time for both units to destroy both depots: 8.83 s, against 24.50 s for the classic AI on the same deploys. the plan changed once for the heavy unit and twice for the fast one, and I check that nobody changes more than three times.

spreading out, which the plan did not ask for

one thing I added that the stage plan does not mention. the field says nothing about who is standing where, so several units deployed together would all follow the gradient to the same attack position and queue on one cell while the others around the building sat free. the classic AI already avoids that, and leaving it out would have made the comparison unfair to the new planner. so the search skips attack cells another unit holds or is heading to, and if nothing is left it falls back to sharing one. it is a spreading rule, not a damage model: it does not know that two units together break a wall faster. that is the job of stage 3.

a terrain the planner has never heard of

the test I care about most is small. the test file registers a new kind of cell that exists only there: passable, and three times slower to walk. no planner code and no simulation code knows about it. the map is a mud band across the path to a depot, with open ground above and below.

speed

the plan asked for one field in under 1 ms on a 40 by 28 map and under 10 ms on 100 by 100. the benchmark builds a map where 30% of the cells are walls of random level and ten buildings sit at random spots, warms up, and times the whole computation:

I reasoned that out and did not try it to watch it fail. and Vitest 5 no longer has a bench function, so the benchmark is a test file that times itself and prints, run with its own config by npm run bench.

where the new planner loses

the depot behind thick walls shows a real weakness. the planner picks, for each unit, the building with the least time left. on that map it destroys the open depot after 12.87 s, where classic reaches its first depot at 49.63 s. but to finish both, the classic AI needs 65.83 s and the time-cost planner 68.20 s. going for the cheap building first was right for the first building and slightly worse for the total, because the unit walks farther overall: to the far depot and then back across the map, instead of the short trip to the near one and then across. choosing the order of buildings is not something this per-building table does. I hoped stage 3 might catch it, but it scores each candidate by when its own target falls, so it has the same limit: on this map the squad planner also finishes in 68.20 s.

the other limits: it is one unit's point of view, so two units hitting the same wall do not help each other yet and the number of free positions around a wall does not cap them. the field is recomputed for every unit at every event, which is fine at 40 by 28 and will need a cache before crowds of 50. the 10% threshold is untuned, and the whole thing has still not run in a browser.

stage 3: a crowd is not a bigger unit

the stage 2 planner treats every unit as alone. deploy six of them at the same spot and each one makes its own decision, and on the L-corner map with a level 3 wall (500 hit points) the six of them broke two different wall cells between them. that took 8.53 s. one shared decision, everyone hits the same wall cell, takes 6.97 s. the goal of stage 3 is that decision: units that stand close together share one plan, their damage adds up, and the planner predicts the time of each option before it picks.

two things make this harder than multiplying the damage by the number of units. a wall can only be hit from the free cells next to it, and one unit per cell, so 100 units do not break a wall 100 times faster. and units do not arrive together: a fast unit is at the wall when a heavy one is still walking.

attack positions become a rule of the simulation

stage 1 already said a cell holds at most one attacker, but in practice a unit whose cell was taken just waited there. now it looks for the nearest free attack position of the same target that it can walk to, reserves it, and walks there. only when none is left does it wait. the search is a small breadth-first walk around the target that respects the corner rule, and the planner uses the exact same function to count positions, so the simulation and its own forecast cannot disagree about what a position is.

the number of positions depends on the side you come from: a wall in a long row has 3 free cells on the near side, not 6. a test puts one wall cell in a corridor one cell wide (1 position) and another in a wall across the whole map (3 positions), sends 3, 8 and then 50 units through it on a fixed route, and reads the time the wall falls:

wall3 units8 units50 units
corridor, 1 position22.03 s22.03 s22.03 s
wall across the map, 3 positions9.00 s9.00 s9.00 s

fifty units are as slow as three, and the time spent hitting (the total minus the 2 s walk) is 20.03 s against 7.00 s, a ratio of 2.86 for 3 positions against 1. there is no infinite speed-up.

how long a wall lasts under a crowd

the function that turns this into a number is short. units start hitting when they arrive, only the first ones that get a position count, and damage is added up piece by piece between arrivals until the hit points are gone.

export function breakTime(hp: number, arrivals: ArrayLike<number>, dps: ArrayLike<number>, slots: number): number { const hitters = Math.min(slots, arrivals.length) let remaining = hp let rate = 0 for (let i = 0; i < hitters; i++) { if (arrivals[i] === Infinity) break rate += dps[i] const nextArrival = i + 1 < hitters ? arrivals[i + 1] : Infinity const damageUntilNext = rate * (nextArrival - arrivals[i]) if (damageUntilNext >= remaining) return arrivals[i] + remaining / rate remaining -= damageUntilNext } return Infinity }

units that arrive with no free position add nothing, and a wall nobody can reach never falls. the same function is used for buildings. the planner finds what can be broken through the registry (the cell is "breakable"), not by asking whether it is a wall.

groups, and a plan with six candidates

units within 5 cells of each other, directly or through a chain of others, form a squad. the squads are re-formed at most five times a second, and a unit that joins or leaves makes its squad decide again. a squad decides like this:

  1. a flow field prices each wall by the damage the squad brings to it: its strongest units, as many as the wall has free positions. from the leader's cell it gives the best free route.
  2. from that field the planner builds up to 6 different candidates: the best route, the full detour with no wall broken, the best route to each of the two next nearest buildings, and the best route again with each wall of the first one banned in turn.
  3. a rollout scores every candidate without running the simulation. it computes when each unit reaches the first wall, gives the first arrivals the positions, gets the time the wall falls, then does the same for the next wall and the building, with arrival times that start after the previous wall is gone.
  4. the lowest estimate wins, and the squad keeps what it has unless the winner is at least 10% faster. if a unit of the squad has already started hitting something, the squad keeps its plans until that falls.

every unit follows the same field, which may only break the walls of the chosen candidate and ends on the same cell as the leader's route. the first two versions of that did not. I will come back to why.

checking it against the real simulation

the test I trust most is an oracle. 200 random 15 by 15 maps, with walls of random level, one to three buildings and a group of one to nine units of mixed types on one cell. for each map the planner lists its candidates and estimates. then each candidate is run in the real simulation with every unit forced onto that candidate's plan and nothing allowed to change it, and the real time until its target falls is measured. two questions: is the planner's pick within 5% of the best real time of the candidates, and how far off is the estimate?

the first run was not good, and the list of what was wrong is the most useful thing in this stage. these are the results after each fix, on the same 200 maps:

two of those were real bugs in the plans, not in the estimate, and I only saw them because the estimate was so far off. in the first version each unit of a squad followed its own path down the field, and on a wall across the map that meant the units spread over different wall cells side by side, each cell with full hit points, instead of hitting one cell together: 11.4 s where the plan said 7.7 s. the fix is that a candidate fixes the walls, and a unit may only break those. the other was that units were planned one by one as they deployed, so the first one, alone, chose the detour before the others existed. now everything that deploys in a step is placed first and planned afterwards.

the honest caveat is that I fixed these by looking at failures among the same 200 maps, so 200 of 200 (worst pick 1.021) is not a fair number. I then ran two fresh sets of 200 maps that I had not looked at: 199 and 198 picks within 5%, worst 1.146 and 1.107, mean estimate error 1.2% both times. on the worst one, a candidate with one wall was estimated at 9.77 s and took 12.33 s, and I did not find out why. the test compares the candidates of the planner with each other, not with every possible plan, and all groups start on one cell.

that is 21 new tests, 52 in total, and all 31 from before pass with identical numbers, including 19.57 s and 9.70 s from stage 1. speed, where I missed

the stage plan asked for a full plan for 10 squads and 50 units on a 40 by 28 map in under 3 ms. the benchmark uses 20% random walls, six buildings, and ten groups of five spread far enough apart to stay separate, and times a full round: every squad decides again, which is what one destroyed wall costs.

so I am about 8 times over the target for a full round. the field alone still takes 0.57 ms on 40 by 28 and 6.0 ms on 100 by 100. one squad needs up to seven fields (six candidates and the final one) and six rollouts, and each rollout has its own search. a round is 25 ms only in the worst case where every squad asks again; a real destroyed wall would matter to only some of them, but I have not built the check that skips the others, and a cache of the fields would come first. the numbers are one machine and one map, 200 rounds each, one run per version, so read them as an order of magnitude.

stage 4: making it visible

everything so far was numbers in a terminal. stage 4 draws the simulation with PixiJS: one canvas, two panels, the classic algorithm on the left and the new one on the right, on the same map with the same units and the same deploy events. the drawing code only reads the worlds. it never writes to them, and src/sim still imports nothing from Pixi.

the loop is the part that has to be right. the simulation runs on a fixed step of 1/30 s. the browser gives me a frame time that changes, so an accumulator turns real time into a whole number of steps, a speed control (0.5x to 4x) scales it, and one long frame (a background tab) is cut to 0.25 s so it cannot turn into a burst of steps. between two steps the view blends each unit's previous and current position, so movement is smooth at any frame rate, and the turning of a unit towards its target is smoothed in the drawing only.

the acceptance test for this is not a picture. the same scenario runs once with frames of exactly 1/60 s and once with a mix of 0.2, 0.013, 0.1 and 0.05 s, and every event and statistic of both worlds has to be the same text. it also has to be the same after Reset. both pass. I checked the picture too, in headless Chrome: on the L-corner map the left panel walks around, the right one breaks the wall and finishes first, and a banner says "the new algorithm is 7.73 s (37%) faster". that is a different map from the 15 by 20 test map (a 40 by 28 grid, wall 22 cells long), so the numbers differ: 21.07 s and 13.33 s.

stage 5: an editor and nine maps

a demo where you can only watch one map is a video. stage 5 adds an editor. the map is one Scenario object, both worlds are built from it, and every editing action is a small pure function on that object (paint a wall of level 1 to 5, erase, place a 1x1, 2x2 or 3x3 building only where the whole footprint is free, add a spawn point, change the hit points of one selected wall or building), with unit tests. any edit resets both worlds. there are also sliders for the speed, damage and hit points of a unit type, the squad radius, the number of candidate plans, the wall hit point multiplier, and a switch that turns group behavior off so the new algorithm plans per unit.

dropping units is the interesting part. a click on either panel puts the same units on the same cell at the same simulated time in both worlds, and the event is written into the scenario's deploy list so that Reset plays it again. the replay test caught a bug here that I would not have seen by eye: I stamped the event with the time of the steps already run, but a world only lands a unit in the next step, so the live run and the replay differed by one step (0.2667 s against 0.2333 s for the same click). the fix is to stamp the time of the next step.

I have to be straight about how the castle and the double layer got their numbers, because they were not first tries. my first castle was a level 2 ring around three buildings, and the new algorithm finished 4.7% behind classic (21.60 s against 20.63 s). both broke almost the same wall at almost the same time. the whole gap was the order of the buildings inside: the same greedy limit as before, a second and a half of extra walking. the first double layer was 1.7% behind for the same reason. so I changed both maps so that the algorithms have a real choice to make: a strong ring with one weak gate, a strong inner ring with one weak door on the far side. that is a fair thing to show, since it is exactly what the algorithm is for, but it is also me building a map where it wins, and the reader should know. the maps where it does not win are in the table too: 5 is a near tie, 7 is a tie by design (one unit), and 9 is a tie because a one-cell corridor leaves no room for either idea (the new algorithm is 0.5% slower, and I left it that way).

the crowd map also showed a difference from the test map: here the switch from walking around to breaking happens at 3 units, not 2, because the map is bigger and the walk around is shorter relative to the wall. a test checks that 1 and 2 units walk around and 3, 4 and 6 break the wall.

stage 6: cartoon style and a debug view

the pictures so far were plain shapes. stage 6 draws everything in code, with no image files: grass with tufts, wall posts that join their neighbors so a line of walls reads as one thing, a different look for each of the five levels (wood, stone, iron, gold, obsidian), cracks that appear at 75%, 50% and 25% of a wall's hit points, a crate, a tower and a keep with a waving flag, and three round characters that hop while they walk, squash and stretch, look where they go and lunge when they hit. hits throw sparks, and a falling wall or building throws dust and chunks, and a building shakes the panel. the particles live in a pool with a hard cap of 360 that has its own random generator, so the decoration cannot touch the simulation.

the debug view is the part I built for myself. a Debug button turns it on, and a click on a unit shows what it decided and why:

  • a heat map of the flow field for that unit type, greener is faster to finish, with a legend in seconds.
  • its route: blue where it walks, green where it breaks a wall, and an X on the wall.
  • colored rings around units that share a plan.
  • the candidate list of its squad: goal, estimated time, number of walls, attack positions counted at each stage, and a mark on the one it follows now.
  • the best walk-around time against the best break-through time, the time per building, and the estimate of the plan it has now against how long the last building really took.
  • the planning time of the latest decision.

two things went wrong on the way. the first version of the candidate list showed a plan with time "never" and no free attack positions for a group that had already started hitting. it is the same effect as in the oracle: asking the planner while units hold the positions gives an answer that is not about the decision. now a group that has started hitting shows a note saying it keeps its plan, and the candidates come back after the target falls. the second was slower: I repainted the heat map (a thousand squares) every frame, and it only changes when the field does. it is now painted when the field is recomputed, twice a second at most.

publishing, and what I did not do

the build uses relative asset paths so it works from any sub-path, and there is a GitHub Actions workflow that installs, runs the tests, builds and publishes to GitHub Pages on every push to main. I wrote it but did not run it: nothing was pushed, and Pages has to be switched on in the repository settings. so I have not seen the published page. what I did check is the production build served locally, in Chrome, including a 700 pixel wide window, where nothing overflows sideways (the readout above each map does sit close to the top of the map there).

the whole thing in one paragraph

the classic rule loses wherever a wall is cheap to break and the way around is long, or where a strong wall stands next to a weak one, and by amounts between 14% and 57% on the maps built for it. it ties where there is nothing to decide, it lost by half a percent on the narrow passage, and in my first versions of two maps it lost by up to 4.7% because of the order of the buildings. the new algorithm predicts its own time to within about 1% on a single unit and 1.2% on average over random groups, and it does that in about 2.4 ms per group, which is 8 times slower than I wanted for a full round of ten groups. every number in this post is from a run I did, and the ones I could not measure (a real graphics card, the published page) are listed as such.

what is still open

the order of buildings is still greedy, and the full planning round for ten groups takes 25 ms against a goal of 3 ms. the count of positions for walls in a row treats earlier walls as gone and cannot know what other groups will occupy. the demo only breaks walls: no gates, traps or defenses, by design. the published page and a real graphics card are unchecked. I wrote the ideas I would try next into a list (a third opponent that breaks walls when the way around is longer than a fixed number of cells, faster planning, saving and sharing maps) and I will go through it before I start another stage.
